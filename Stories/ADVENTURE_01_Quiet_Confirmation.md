### `n01` THE ORDER

**Titan gives one order, and it is not "destroy it."**

Rain comes down on the furnace canyon hard enough to ring on the gantry grating, note after note, all night long. A hundred meters below, the crucibles tip and pour, and the molten light comes up through the steel mesh in bars, so the whole walkway is a cage of orange stripes laid over the dark. Chimneys write smoke sideways across the wind. Nothing here stops for weather. Nothing here stops for anything at all. The Foundry has not slept in two hundred years, and the robots who work it gave up hearing the roar a long time ago.

Titan rises into that light the way a wall rises. Blue and yellow under the soot, forge-iron slabs where a gardener's plating used to be. His shoulders run so hot that the rain does not land on them so much as vanish, hissing, in little torn rags of steam. When he shifts his weight the servos in his back groan like a door in a deep building. Panther is already beside him. He arrived without a sound, because arriving without a sound is the whole of what Panther is for, and now he waits, and says nothing, and lets the rain fill the gap.

Titan holds out one hand, palm flat, and keeps it there. It is the size of a door and it is completely empty. No weapon in it. No map. No second unit standing ready at the gate. Nothing that could be prised off a fallen body afterwards and traced back up the chain to the robot standing on this gantry. Rainwater pools in the great open palm and spills over the edge of it, and Titan does not move the hand at all.

Panther's single amber visor slit narrows a fraction as he reads it. Furnace light slides across the dark navy of his helmet. He has taken a thousand orders from this hand and every one of them came with something in it — a target list, a crest, a charge, a name. An empty hand is not a smaller order. An empty hand is an order that has been carefully cleaned.

"Go to their garden," Titan says. "Confirm the green is real."

A pause the size of a building.

"Quietly."

The huge fingers fold shut on nothing. Rainwater runs off the knuckles in ropes and falls a hundred meters into the orange dark below. That is the entire briefing.

Panther answers the way he has answered every order for eighty years, which is not at all. He turns and runs the length of the gantry, boots ringing on grating, and across his back the jet panels flex open a hand's width out of pure habit. He shuts them cold before they finish opening. A jet can be heard for ten kilometres on a night like this, and quietly is not a note scribbled on the order — quietly is the order. So the fastest thing he owns stays folded on his back all night like a coat he is not allowed to wear. It will cost him before dawn. He knows that while he is doing it. He does it anyway.

The stairs are at the far end and the far end is the wrong direction, so he goes over the rail instead. Eighty meters of wet brick. Two kicks off the wall to bleed his speed, each one taking a bite out of two-hundred-year-old mortar, furnace vents lighting him orange as he drops past them, wind tearing up the whole length of him. He lands in a crater of dust and standing water and takes the fall through both knees. He is running east before the splash comes down behind him.

The flats begin where the slag heaps end. Gray to the horizon, cracked into plates, puddles standing in every seam. A kilometre out, on ground where nothing has grown since before he was built, he stops dead. Something behind him moved when he moved. He turns around slowly, the way you turn when you already know. Far back, high on the lit foundry wall, a second thin shadow peels itself off the glare and drops — and it does not use the ladder either, and it does not open a jet either, and it lands somewhere out in the dark without making one sound worth hearing. Whoever that is got the same order he did. Probably word for word. Probably from the same empty hand. Rain fills the flats. Nothing else moves anywhere in them.

### `n02` THE PIPELINE

*from: `n01`*

**The dead pipe is warm. Dead things are not warm.**

The pipeline was a fuel line before the Leaving, and it has been dead ever since. Its mouth sits in a bank of gray slag with a grate bolted across it, and the bolts are two centuries of rust holding hands. Three orange claws hook under the edge and take the whole grate off in one pull. Rust flakes spin away into the rain. Nobody sees you underground — that is the entire argument for what he is about to do, and it is a good argument, and it is the reason he does not look for a second one.

He drops in feet first. Black water at the bottom, up past his shins, cold enough to make his plating tick as it cools. The light strips along his arms wake and throw a glow onto ribbed walls curving away into the dark in both directions, exactly the same in both directions, which is a thing that is going to matter later. He picks east. He goes down onto his elbows, because the pipe was never built for a robot to stand up in, and he starts to crawl.

It is fast, and it is ugly, and it is completely invisible. Elbow, claw, elbow, claw, rib after rib strobing past his shoulders, water spraying off every push and coming back down on his helmet in a warm dirty rain of his own making. He counts the joints as they go by, because there is nothing else down here to count and a stalker who stops counting starts inventing. Forty-one. Forty-two. Forty-three.

Halfway across, he stops mid-push. The metal is humming against his visor. Not the storm — the storm is a long way up and sounds like a long way up, a soft drumming through a hundred tons of dirt. This is close, and steady, and coming out of the wall itself. He lays one claw flat against the pipe to be certain, and the answer comes up through his arm and settles in him like a swallowed stone.

The pipe is warm. Warm means running. Nothing down here has run since the humans left.

Then the dark ahead of him wakes up. Red eyes come on in ranks, dozens of them, low to the water, and start crawling toward him along the curve of the pipe. He hears them a half second after he sees them: a dry ticking, hundreds of small legs finding purchase on old steel, the sound rain would make if rain had feet. He presses himself flat into the silt and stops being anything at all.

The first one ticks past inches from his visor. Four legs, a body the size of his fist, a thin laser stitching the rusted wall as it goes — measuring, logging, entirely uninterested in the robot lying in the mud underneath it. There is a crest stamped on its back. It is the Foundry's. It is his. Then the rest of the swarm comes over him, and around him, and briefly across the back of his neck, and not one of them so much as slows down.

Not a patrol. A survey. Dozens of machines mapping a route toward the Warden district with the flat patience of things that have been at it for weeks and expect to be at it for weeks more. Nobody mentioned this to the stalker they sent out alone tonight. That leaves two possibilities: either nobody thought he needed to know, or somebody decided he should not. What he notices, lying in the silt with a survey drone walking over his shoulder, is that he is not surprised by the second one. He tells himself he will be surprised about it later.

Ahead, the pipe finally ends. There is a hatch of cyan light cut into a circle by the pipe mouth, and Warden voices come down the tube on the damp air, ordinary and close and complaining about the rain. Somebody says the sled is late. Somebody else says the sled is always late. Water drips. They sound like a crew at the end of a long shift, because that is exactly what they are, and nothing about that is useful to him.

Behind him, one drone stops dead. He hears the change before he sees it — one tick missing out of hundreds. Its eye rotates. It hunts along the pipe floor, past the silt, past the standing water, and finds him lying there with rust on his shoulders. It holds. Ahead of him: light, voices, a way out. Behind him: one small red eye, wide open, looking straight at him, not moving at all.

### `n03` THE SCAR

*from: `n01`*

**The crawler's tracks are still hot. They should not exist yet.**

He drops into the scar and puts one claw straight down into a tread rut a meter deep. Steam curls up around his fingers. Rain hits the churned earth beside them and flashes to vapour before it can pool. The ground is warm — not sun-warm, there has been no sun for two days; machine-warm, the deep held heat of something enormous that came through here and did not come through long ago.

He rises out of the rut and looks down the length of the road he chose. The scar runs arrow-straight to the horizon, a kilometre wide, gouged through a dead gray city by something the size of a mountain. Buildings stand sheared in half along both rims, rooms hanging open to the weather like a doll's house with its front off. Wind comes down it in one long unbroken push. It is the fastest way to the Warden district. It is also the most exposed ground in the district, and he knew that when he picked it.

He runs. Flat out, the way he almost never gets to run, a rooster-tail of wet dust thrown up behind him and grit spraying off both heels. The rut walls slide past on either side, higher than his head in places, then lower, then gone. Halfway down the first stretch he passes a car pressed flat as a coin in the mud, and there is still paint on it, blue paint, two hundred years old and shining in the rain because the crawler polished it on its way over.

Under his feet the ground keeps saying the same thing. Heat shimmer ripples off the earth between strides. These tracks are hours old — not days, hours — and the crawler's published route puts it a full day west of here. He has read that route. Every unit in the Foundry has read that route, and so, he thinks, have the Wardens, because a route like that is not a secret, it is a warning. Which means the garden has a day less than anybody counting on the schedule believes it has. Including the crew that found the green. Including whoever else is out here tonight walking toward it.

Seen from the rim he would be nothing at all: one small orange spark crossing the floor of a gouge wide enough to lose a town in, thunder going somewhere behind the clouds, the dead city standing up on both sides to watch him do it. He runs anyway. Whatever the schedule says, the ground says otherwise, and the ground has no reason to lie.

Still running, he brings the route glyph up on his forearm — a thin line drawn across a map of the valley, chirping softly as it loads — and holds it against what is under his boots. It does not match. Not by a rounding error. By a day. Somebody moved the crawler up. Somebody filed the change properly, in neat columns, with a stamp on it, because everything in the Foundry is filed properly. And somebody left his name off the distribution. He is Titan's stalker. There is no list in the Foundry he is supposed to be off.

He is still turning that over when something shifts in the dark ahead of him. A shape comes up over the lip of the scar, servos growling, too big to be weather and too deliberate to be a slide of loose ground. It settles. It waits. It has been waiting a while, he thinks, and it did not pick that spot by accident, because that spot can see the whole floor of the scar in both directions.

Then the dark opens one white eye. The searchlight snaps on and sweeps the floor of the scar in a long slow arc, and where it crosses him it throws his shadow twenty meters up the canyon wall — a black cut-out robot running along a ruin with nowhere in the world to go.

The beam catches him mid-stride, and stops. So does he. He does not run. Everything in him built for exactly that is telling him to, and he does not, because there is a kilometre of open ground in every direction and not one thing anywhere in it taller than his knee, and running is how you tell a searchlight it has found something worth chasing. So he stands in the middle of a kilometre of nothing, lit up hard white, rain steaming off his shoulders, and lets the light look at him.

### `n04` THE SLED

*from: `n02`*

**He stows away in enemy cargo to find out what Wardens haul at midnight.**

The sled comes up the flooded street at walking pace: a flat gray slab of a thing on six wheels, cyan lanterns swinging off its rails, water peeling away from the tires in slow white wings. It throws its light around like a machine with nothing at all to hide, which is either honesty or the best trick he has seen all night. Three Wardens ride the front rail, hunched under the rain, arguing quietly about whose turn it is to do the gate. Not one of them is looking back.

He goes over the tailboard between one lantern swing and the next. Nothing squeaks. Nothing shifts. One soft footfall on wet deck plate, and he is aboard the enemy. There is a hand-painted number on the tailboard, and under it, smaller, in careful letters somebody clearly took their time over: TRAY RUN 4. He files that and does not know yet why it bothers him.

He folds himself down into the gap between two crates — knees to chest, claws tucked in, head low — and then stops being a robot and becomes cargo. Running lights strobe through the slats and slide across his orange plating in stripes. The crates creak against each other with every turn. A wheel finds a pothole and the whole deck bangs hard enough to lift him a centimetre off it, and he comes back down without a sound. Up front, somebody laughs at something. Nobody turns around.

This is the part of the work he is good at, and the part nobody at the Foundry has ever once asked him about. Ride in the enemy's freight and let the manifest tell you who the enemy is. Armies lie in speeches. Nobody lies in a cargo bay at midnight. He hooks one claw under the nearest lid and lifts it a finger's width, rainwater running off his nails into the dark inside, and he already knows what he is going to find, because there is exactly one thing a faction hauls across a district at midnight in the rain.

He is wrong.

Soil trays. The gap widens and the light gets in and it is dirt — tray after tray of it, damp, raked flat and level, stacked to the sled roof. Somebody combed the surface of every single one. There are finger marks along the edges where they did it. He lifts the second lid. Trays. The third. Trays. Fifty crates of dirt rocking gently with the road, and not one round of ammunition anywhere on this sled.

Between the racks, strapped down with more care than anything else aboard, sit sealed drums of clean water. They slosh with the motion of the road. The straps creak tight over them at every corner, and somebody has padded the buckles with rag so they will not scar the drum. Whoever loaded this sled treated the water as the valuable part, and the water is the one thing here he cannot argue with — clean water costs more than a weapon, and both sides know it, and only one side is hauling it out of town in the dark.

The sled grinds on through flooded streets, lanterns squeaking on their hooks, cyan light sliding over dead gray houses with their windows long gone. Panther turns it over the whole way and gets the same answer every time he turns it. Every night, all night, out in rain that eats plating, this crew hauls dirt and clean water to ground that has not grown anything in a hundred years. There is no weapon in it. There is no advantage in it. Nobody is even watching them do it — he is watching them do it, and he does not count. It refuses to mean anything. It goes on refusing, mile after mile, and that is somehow worse than a warehouse full of guns would have been.

Then the front wheels drop into a rut.

The deck tips. The stack goes, all at once, crates sliding with a crash, and he grabs for a strap in the dark and comes away holding a tray instead — wet dirt, combed level, cradled in three orange claws. He lands flat on his back in a floodlit yard. Every lantern in it swings onto him at the same moment. Fifty of them. Fifty Wardens, one Foundry stalker lying in their mud, holding their dirt. Nobody says a word. The floodlights buzz.

### `n05` THE DRONE'S MAP

*from: `n02`*

**The little drone is carrying somebody's secret, and it is not Titan's.**

The drone never gets a chance to run. One claw comes out of the dark and pins it flat to the pipe wall, and it goes off like a wind-up toy — four little legs scrabbling at nothing, its whole body buzzing against the steel, all of that effort going into a struggle that would not move a robot's finger. Panther holds it there and lets it tire itself out. It takes eleven seconds. He counts them, because counting is what he does with the parts of a job that are only waiting.

Up close, the red sensor eye whirs in and out, hunting focus, and finds nothing but a wall of orange plating filling its entire view. It has never had to look at anything this big before. It has spent its short life measuring rust. Down the pipe, the rest of the swarm ticks steadily away into the dark without breaking step. Not one of them turns around. Not one of them comes back for it. Whatever the Foundry taught these things, it did not include coming back, and Panther puts that thought on the shelf with the other things he has learned about his own side tonight and does not take any of them down to look at. Black water runs past his elbows. Then he walks a claw tip around the seam and peels the casing back like a rind. It comes away in one strip, with a small pop, and light spills out of the break.

The map unspools out of the broken shell into his hands and lights the whole curve of the pipe. The Warden district, street by street. Gridded. Measured. He turns it with a claw and it turns for him, obedient the way maps are. The recycler yard is ranked high. The bridges are ranked high. A whole row of houses is ranked at nothing, because houses are what the Foundry thinks houses are worth. Every block has a number beside it in a tidy column, and the heading over the column is TAKE ORDER, and somebody has already gone down that list and decided what gets eaten first.

And in the middle of all of it, drawn tight and drawn on purpose, is one circle around a single small backyard in sector forty-one.

He does not move for a while. The map has been walked — not swept once from the air, walked, and then walked again, with corrections on it, tidy notes in a small careful hand, a route marked and afterwards improved. Somebody stood in that yard. Somebody counted the fence posts. Days ago. And then said nothing to him, which he could live with, and said nothing to the robot who sent him out here tonight with an empty hand and one word, which he cannot.

Quietly, Titan told him, as though quietly were still a thing that belonged to Panther.

Every survey order carries an authorization stamp. He slides the map sideways until the corner comes up, pushes his visor in until the mark fills the slit, and braces himself for the crest he has taken every order of his life from.

It is not Titan's. It is horned. It is Samurai's. And the date on it is six days old — three days before the Wardens ever came to the gates, four days before Bolt held a tablet up in the rain and asked a mountain to look at a leaf.

Under his claw, the little drone stops fighting. He feels it happen: the scrabbling quits all at once and the body goes stiff and purposeful instead, the way a machine does when it stops trying to escape and starts doing its actual job. Its eye comes up hot red. Then it opens its throat and screams his position at the sky — a hard flat tone that fills the pipe end to end and goes tearing away up the tube toward the hatch of cyan light and the Warden voices, and it does not stop for breath, because it does not breathe.

Panther does not let go. He has already worked out that letting go would not help, and that crushing it would not help either, and that the only thing left in the pipe tonight that is still quiet is him.

### `n06` THE SQUALL

### `n07` SEEN ON PURPOSE

*from: `n03`*

**He lets them scan him — and the wrong voice says hello.**

Running is what a spy does. So Panther does not run. He walks. Straight up the middle of the beam and into the worst of the light, boots going down into the mud and coming out of it without hurrying, and the glare gets whiter and hotter with every step until there is nothing else left in the world. Ten meters short of it he stops, opens all three claws on both hands, and holds them out flat and empty. Then he stops moving entirely — not the way a robot waiting is still, the way a rock is still. Rain sheets off his orange plating. Behind the visor, quietly, he counts.

The outrider grows out of the glare as it wades closer: six legs working through the mud on hydraulics that whine at every step, a body like a squat furnace, and a lamp where a face should be. Gun pods sit live on both shoulders, tracking him in small constant corrections. It stops near enough that the rain hitting its hot lamp housing turns to steam on the way in, and the steam blows back across Panther and he does not blink, because he does not have anything to blink with and because blinking is not the point.

A scan line crawls down him from helmet to heel. It finds the Foundry mark stamped into his chest plate and slows over it, reading. Then it climbs all the way back up and reads the mark a second time. The amber visor slit does not move. The mark is the only interesting thing about him and he needs it to stay that way, so he gives the machine nothing else to be curious about: no weapon up, no channel open, no reason.

An outrider is not clever. It has one question and the question is whose. It cannot ask why a stalker is eleven kilometres east of anywhere he has business being, in weather like this, on foot, with his jet folded cold. Panther has built the last eighty years out of standing perfectly still inside the gap between the question a machine can ask and the question somebody ought to.

The gun pods unlock and fold away, satisfied. The searchlight dips off his face and lays itself down on the ground between them. He is exactly what he says he is. That is the whole trick, and the trick works every time, and it has never once made him feel clever. It only ever makes him feel like a stamped piece of metal that a bigger stamped piece of metal has agreed to believe.

Then a channel opens in his head, and the carrier tone is wrong. Outriders do not carry channels. Outriders bark, if they say anything at all. This is a clean line coming in from somewhere else in the district entirely, and he knows the voice before it finishes its first word, and his head tilts one fraction of a degree, which is more reaction than he has given anything all night.

Samurai. Courteous. Unhurried. Pleased in the particular way a robot is pleased when a piece turns up on a square he was already watching. So glad to find Panther out here tonight. So early, too.

"Good," Samurai says. "Ride with the team. You will want to see what we do at dawn."

A Foundry transport comes grinding up in tank mode, ember lights burning along both flanks, treads throwing mud in two long fans. There is no burn scheduled tonight. He has read the same orders Titan reads — he reads most of them before Titan does — and there is nothing on any of them, anywhere, about dawn.

### `n08` THE ROOFLINE

*from: `n04`, `n05`, `n06`, `n07`*

**From up here he can see what the Wardens are guarding, and it makes no sense at all.**

Panther takes the rooftops, because a rooftop is a road nobody maintains and nobody watches. He goes up a drainpipe at the edge of the district and does not come down again for a kilometre. A chimney stack comes at him out of the rain and goes under him without costing a stride. A skylight slides past on his left, black and blind, an old window into somebody's old life. Rain comes off his shoulders in sheets and comes off the ridge tiles in a second, thinner rain that blows sideways along the roof and into his knees. A wet slate slips out from under one heel and clatters away down the pitch into the dark, and the next stride has caught him before he has finished falling. He does not slow for it. Sector forty-one crawls closer through the storm. Somewhere around the third block he starts noticing what he is not hearing.

He leans out over the gutter line as he runs and looks down at the street going past underneath, and the street is wrong. No patrols. Every doorway dark. Not one lantern hung out anywhere along the whole length of it, and Wardens hang lanterns the way other machines vent heat, constantly, without deciding to, whether anybody needs the light or not. Halfway down, somebody has left a soil tray out on a windowsill in the weather. The dirt in it is raked flat. It has been watered tonight; he can see the little runnels where the can went over. Nothing has ever grown in that tray and somebody watered it anyway, and then went out, and has not come back for it.

The footbridge is empty. The water tower is empty, and the water tower is the one place he would put a watcher if this district were his to hold, because one robot up there sees eleven streets at once. The gutters are overflowing and nobody is counting the rain. Every Warden district he has ever crossed counts the rain, chalks the number on a board by the door, argues about the number. A whole neighbourhood left standing open like a door somebody forgot to shut.

"Quietly," Titan had said, with his hand out and nothing in it. Quietly is easy tonight. Quietly is being handed to him, and he does not like being handed things. Panther runs a full block along the ridge tiles waiting for the trap to close, and no trap closes, and somehow that is worse than an ambush, because an ambush he would understand. Then he crests the last long parapet and finds out where everybody went.

They are in a backyard. All of them, fifty robots packed into one small square of ground behind a house, standing out in the rain in a ring, shoulder to shoulder, facing inward at something the roofline is too high and too close to let him see. He goes flat on the wet lead and works along the edge on his elbows and reads the shape of it the way he was built to read shapes. Not dug in. No trench. No barricade. No firing line, no overlap, nobody covering the alley mouth, which is the only sensible way in and is standing wide open behind them. His visor tracks slowly round the circle and counts dented shoulders and patched arms and a garden rake and a second garden rake and lanterns held up high in the wet by robots whose arms must be aching by now.

Nobody talks. That is the part that snags on him. Fifty robots standing in a storm and not one of them complaining, not shifting their feet, not asking how much longer this goes on. He has waited in ambush with Foundry units that could not manage four quiet minutes.

From straight above, the ring is closed tight and perfectly round, and the middle of it is a patch of dark ground he cannot see into. Fifty robots making a fence out of themselves for a patch of dirt. He turns the sentence over twice and cannot get anything useful out of it, and he has been trained to get something useful out of everything. So he leans out further, to see past their shoulders, and he puts his weight on a parapet that two hundred years of acid rain got to first.

The mortar goes to wet paste under his claw without a sound. Then the brick lets go in one long sheet, the whole top course of it, the rain gutter tearing off the boards with a shriek that carries, and brick and iron and Panther come down together out of the dark toward the ring of lanterns.

### `n09` THE SHELF

*from: `n04`*

**He walks into the enemy's home, and the enemy hands him a cloth.**

He ruins his own paint first.

It has to be done properly, so he does it properly. One short, deliberate shove of his shoulder into a doorframe in the alley, hard enough that the clean orange edge folds into a dent and a flake of paint drops into the puddle at his feet. Then he goes down on one knee in the wet and works gray street dust into every seam he has. Up the arms. Along the shins. Into the joints of his neck, where real dust always ends up on a robot who has been outside all night. When he stands, nothing about him shines and nothing about him says Foundry, and the only thing left that could give him away is his hands, so he curls the claws under and keeps them there.

Head down. Shoulders round. He falls in at the back of a crowd of small mismatched Wardens shuffling in out of the rain, close enough to the one in front that he moves when it moves, and the doorway takes him without a ripple. Nobody in this building looks twice at one more tired robot glad of a roof. That is the whole trick of him and it has never once failed.

Inside it is warm and crowded and loud with the clatter of wet feet on a stone floor. Water runs off forty hulls at once and goes away down a drain that somebody has kept clear for two centuries. An old scratched unit, half his height, missing most of one ear panel, reaches up and presses a polishing cloth into his three-clawed hand without once looking at the hand he is putting it into, and points a bent finger away down the long wall. The shelf runs the whole length of it.

Cores. Dormant robots, dozens of them, laid out in a row and sleeping faint cyan, giving off the slow pulse a core makes when it is only waiting: in, hold, out, the same rhythm as something asleep. Each one wears a hand-lettered name tag tied on with wire. Each one is dust-free. Each one is gleaming, and in a building this old there is exactly one way that happens. Somebody walks this wall every single day with a cloth and refuses to give up on any of them. Some of the tags have been written twice, he notices. The ink went faint, and years later a different hand went over the same letters again, and that hand never met the robot whose name it was copying out. So Panther dusts the shelf.

Slow circles, the way the others do it, elbow going, head down. Further along the row three small Wardens work the same slow circles beside him and nobody says anything at all, and under the noise of the rain outside the room is quiet enough that he can hear the cloths. His own plating looks back at him out of the polished casings, orange under the gray, dented, wrong. He keeps working. His own side put half these robots on this wall. He knows the numbers, he has helped make some of the numbers, and he is the one they send to do the quiet part of it, the part nobody writes down afterwards.

The word the reports use is shelved. Nobody ever wrote down has a name tag. Nobody ever wrote down is polished every morning by a robot with one ear, in a building with no spare parts in it, by robots who cannot rebuild a single one of them and go on cleaning them anyway. He is thinking about that, and about how warm it is in here, when the cloth stops moving.

It stops before he understands why. The claw has locked itself. Lantern light shivers along the row and settles on the tag under his hand, and the letters sit there and go on being exactly what they are no matter how long he looks at them.

It is the name of the partner he lost two winters ago. The one he filed as a loss and closed the same night. The one he was told the Wardens had taken the core of, and never once asked after, because asking is not a thing his side does. The cloth slips out of his claws and lands on the floor, and two meters down the row a small scratched Warden looks up at the sound.

### `n10` THE RACE

*from: `n05`*

**He beats the alarm to sector forty-one, and somebody has already beaten him.**

The drone's scream is still climbing out of the pipe behind him when he hits the street, and after that it is only running.

Behind him the district starts to wake. Siren light blooms orange in the blocks he has already left, one and then another and then a whole row of them, all of it chasing a robot who is not there any more. Four blocks. Then five. Nothing moves. Rain falls into a dead lawn. The ruined treehouse stands black against the storm on its dead tree, one broken rail, one loose board turning slowly on a single nail in the wind. No lanterns anywhere. No ring of Wardens. Nobody at all, in the one yard in this district that anybody should be standing in tonight. He is first.

He is not first.

The wire fence is cut. Not rusted through, not bent open by two hundred winters of weather, not pushed down by a falling branch. Cut. The sheared ends are still bright silver where the tool went through them, no rain-spotting on the metal yet, and they are still swinging a little, the way wire swings for a while after something has passed between it. He watches the water instead, because water gives him a number. It is coming up the inside of the print in a slow gray creep, filling the tread lines one at a time, and the print is not soft yet, which means minutes. Not hours. Minutes.

Fifty Wardens are somewhere in this district tonight and none of them noticed a fence being cut in their own back garden, which is work, real work, the kind he would have signed. ### `n11` THE GRID

*from: `n06`*

**He kills the district's power, and something starts screaming in the dark.**

The junction box on the corner is older than the war and nobody has opened it since the humans left. Two hundred years of paint has run down the front of it in one long gray drip and dried there. Three claws hook under the cover and take the whole thing off the wall in a single pull. Sparks come up in a fountain and go all over his orange plating, and he stands in the middle of them and does not flinch, because sparks are nothing to a robot. ### `n14` THE YARD

*from: `n08`, `n09`, `n10`*

**He gets exactly what he wanted: the yard, alone, no witnesses.**

Fifty lanterns, and every single one of them is pointed the wrong way.

The whole ring is out at the far fence with its back to him, shoulder to shoulder, lights raised, guarding a road that nobody is coming up. Panther watches them from the alley for a good while, because this is exactly the kind of gift that turns out to be a trap, and he has walked into two in his life and remembers both. It is not a trap. They are simply looking at the horizon they decided the trouble would arrive from, and the trouble has come over the roofs behind them instead. Down the road they are watching, nothing moves at all. Rain fills the ruts and overflows them. He steps through the gap behind them and does not even need to hurry.

Then he is inside, and low, almost flat, sliding across the yard with the rain sheeting off his back, and there is grass under his hands.

Grass. Wet green blades, bent over with the weight of the rain and springing back up when the drop rolls off, dozens of them, hundreds, a whole ragged carpet of the stuff. He has only ever seen pictures. He parts them with the tips of three claws, one blade at a time, careful not to bend a single one, and it takes him longer to cross four meters of lawn than it took him to cross the district. Nobody watching would understand what is slow about it. He could not explain it either. He has crossed a burning district tonight at a speed nobody in the Foundry can match, and here he is on his elbows in a flooded lawn, moving one blade of grass out of the way of another blade of grass so that neither of them gets stepped on. He puts his weight through his knuckles instead of his palms, the way you do on a roof you do not trust.

Then his hands stop moving altogether, because the ground under the grass is wrong.

It is clean. Not swept clean, not washed clean by two days of rain. Clean the way nothing on this planet has been clean since before he was built. He puts a claw into it and lifts a little of it and it comes apart soft and dark between his nails and it does not smell of rust, and everything on Earth smells of rust. So he rolls a sensor pulse out across the yard.

The depth readings come tumbling up the inside of his visor and keep tumbling. Ten centimeters, clean. Fifty, clean. A meter, clean. Two meters, clean. Every other handful of dirt on this world is two hundred years of poison stacked in layers you can read like a book, and here the book is blank, page after page, until the scale runs out of numbers, flickers, and blinks empty at him. He runs it again because instruments lie. It blinks empty at him again. Then the pulse finishes painting the shape of it, and the shape is a circle.

Forty meters across, edge to edge, true the whole way round, with him crouched tiny at the rim of it. Nature does not make circles. Rain does not make circles. Poison does not politely stop in a smooth curve four paces short of a fence. Somebody knelt down in this yard with their hands in the dirt and cleaned a poisoned world one handful at a time, working outward, and stopped where they ran out of night, and came back and did it again, and went on doing it while wars started and finished and started again.

At the boundary he finds the proof of it and wishes he had not. Little scooped grooves in the clay, thumb-wide, thousands of them, overlapping, worn smooth. Handmarks. The same two hands, over and over, for longer than he has existed. Two hundred years. Every day. Nobody told. Nothing to show for it but a lawn, in a district that gets a strip-miner in the morning.

He is still crouched there with his claw in the clean dirt when the ruined treehouse above him creaks and one board settles under a weight that was not on it a moment ago, and a little dust comes down through the rain, and then nothing else moves at all.

### `n15` THE OLD WARDEN

**The old one has known exactly what he is since he walked in the door.**

He has simply kept hold of a Foundry stalker's arm and said out loud, in a normal voice, what he can see. That is a thing Panther has no procedure for. There is a procedure for being caught. ### `n17` CUT LOOSE

*from: `n12`*

**He is alone in a dead city with a torch and no side.**

The transport is a bonfire in the rain. Torch racks topple off both flanks one after another, gas bottles venting white through the downpour, and six scorched heavies stagger out into the flooded street and stand there in it, hulls steaming, helmets down, not one of them looking at any of the others. He holds it out over the water and lets go, and it drops in and goes out with a small polite hiss, and the puddle goes from orange to black, and that is the last quiet thing that happens tonight.

Then he runs. Down a canyon of dead storefronts, water exploding off his heels, one orange streak repeating away down two hundred years of blank shop glass. The jet panels across his back stay clamped shut and cold, because loud is what gets you caught, and being not-loud is the entire thing he is for. In a window going past there is still a price painted on the inside of the glass, careful white numbers with a little curl under them, and he reads it without meaning to, and then it is behind him.

Nine strides later it stops mattering. Mid-stride, his own chest plate lights up.

He did not switch it on. He rakes at it with three claws hard enough to lift the paint in curls, and the light climbs back up out of the scratches brighter than before, pulsing traitor-bright through the rain, throwing his shadow across the storefronts in a slow patient heartbeat and singing exactly where he is to every Foundry ear inside ten kilometres. DESERTER.

An hour. A full hour before he put anything through anybody's fuel line. Before the transport. Before the torch was ever handed to him handle-first. ### `n18` THE TORCH

**He takes the torch, and the whole team relaxes. That was their mistake.**

The scorched heavy thumbs the striker and the torch goes up white and roaring, boiling the rain off itself in a sleeve of steam, and he turns it around and offers it handle-first — the way you hand a tool to somebody you have already finished making up your mind about. Another finally bothers to shut the ramp, which he has not bothered to do for the last ten minutes. A robot holding a torch is a robot who has picked a side, and six of them stop watching him at the same instant, and that is the single most useful thing any of them do all night.

That was their mistake.

He walks. Past the burn team. Past the transport with its treads ticking as they cool in the wet. Past the cut wire fence and the rusted tricycle grown into it, torch held out level at his hip, sparks spitting off the nozzle and dying the instant they touch the mud. Nobody stops him. Nobody so much as turns a helmet, because he is walking in exactly the direction they were all about to walk in, and there is nothing in the world less interesting than somebody doing what you expected.

Then he lowers the head of the torch into the dirt and keeps going, fast, and the night starts to hiss.

The fastest line to sector forty-one runs straight across the recycler floor, and Panther takes it. Forty tons of gear-driven steel, wound up at the start of a shift and turning until it decides to stop, folds him forward into the silt with his claws still open and still reaching for nothing in particular, and then goes on grinding above him without him, exactly as patiently as before.

The cyan strips along his spine gutter out one at a time, shoulders down, like street lamps being switched off from the far end. It does not hurt. That is the strange thing he has time to notice. It is only the lights going out in a building at the end of a shift, room by room, and somebody at the door with a hand on the switch.

The last thing he logs, tilting, dimming, is three small lanterns going out behind him. One. Then one. Then one. He did not turn back for them. The picture narrows to a dot at the centre of everything, and the dot closes.

Then: a cloth. Humming. Light.

Somebody is polishing him. Slow circles over his shoulder plating, patient as weather, working grit out of a seam the way you work a mark off a tabletop, going back over the same spot four and five times because it is not quite right yet — and whoever is holding the cloth is humming with no tune in it at all, the way robots hum when they have stopped noticing that they are doing it.

A hand-lettered name tag hangs beside his shoulder on a bent wire. The ink has gone soft and brown. The corners are furred and rounded off from being picked up, turned over, and set down again every single day for a very long time.

His amber visor lights for the first time in decades. It comes up slow, blinks twice, holds. The dark navy helmet lifts a few degrees and the whole world outside it is far too loud and far too bright and full of running water, and it takes him four seconds to understand that the noise is watering cans.

He boots into a stadium he has never seen in daylight. Rank after rank of soil trays run the whole length of the field, hundreds of them, and every single tray is carrying green — real green, shoots standing up in rows with the water still beaded on them, small dented Wardens walking the aisles between them with cans that drip and drip. Steam comes off the wet trays wherever the light lands. Somewhere out in the middle of it, somebody laughs at something.

"Forty years," the old one says, without looking up. "Near enough. You were third from the end for a good while. Then we shuffled you along." He turns the cloth over to a clean part of it. ### `n16` THE TREEHOUSE

*from: `n10`, `n14`*

**Someone is waiting in the treehouse, and he did not bring a weapon either.**

The tree died before the war did. Two hundred years of sour weather have stripped it back to gray bone, and the house nailed up in its crook is in worse shape than the tree: four walls of warped board, hammered together by somebody very small who was not much good with a hammer, sitting crooked now like a hat nobody straightened. He notices, and puts the noticing somewhere he does not have to look at it.

He hooks the broken rail with three claws and rolls over it onto the boards without a sound, because he sets his weight on the joist and not on the plank. Eighty years of climbing into places he was not invited into. He comes up in a crouch in the dark with all three claws spread, turned toward the middle of the room, ready for whatever is about to happen.

In the far corner, a wick catches.

The flame stands up, wobbles once, steadies, and pushes a small ring of light out across two hundred years of dust. Samurai is already sitting in it. Cross-legged on the boards, ice-blue and charcoal, the horned crest a finger's width from the low roof, hands folded on his knees like a robot waiting for a bus that is not late yet. He does not turn his head. He does not need to. He has picked the one corner of this room from which he can watch both ways out at once, and Panther works that out in the same half second he works out that there are only two.

Then he sees the water, or rather he sees where the water is not. Samurai is dry. Not damp, not drying — dry, all the way down to the seams of his knuckles, while Panther stands there shedding the storm in ropes off his elbows. Dry means hours. Hours means Samurai climbed up into a child's playhouse before the rain even started, folded himself into the dark of it, and sat there without a lamp, waiting to find out who the Foundry would send.

Neither of them moves. The rain hammers the roof between them and finds its way through in three places. Water runs off Panther's claws, hits the low board, and starts building a puddle one drop at a time, and for a long while that is the only thing either of them contributes to the conversation. On the wall behind Samurai's shoulder there are four pencil marks in a stack, each one a little higher than the last, the top one no higher than Samurai's knee. Panther looks at them for exactly as long as he can afford to.

They slide across the little table — a child's table, knee-high, with two centuries of dust lying on it like felt — and cut two clean tracks through it, and stop on a single rain-spotted sheet of paper. Samurai turns it around so that it faces the right way up for his guest, and pushes it into the lamplight, and lifts one finger off it.

Crayon. Faded down to whispers of wax, but all of it still there. A stick-figure girl in a triangle dress, one arm out sideways. ### `n19` THE GREEN

*from: `n13`, `n14`, `n15`*

**He crossed a dead sea in acid rain to confirm it. It is the size of his thumb.**

Two leaves. That is all of it. Two leaves and a stem he could snap by breathing on it.

He stands over it for a while and does nothing at all. The rain is bigger than the plant is. Every drop that lands knocks it flat into the mud, and every time, it comes back up — slow, crooked, stubborn, like something shrugging — and then it gets knocked flat again, and comes back up again. It has plainly been doing this all night. It clearly intends to go on doing it. Nobody has explained the odds to it, and it would not understand them if they did.

Around it, forty meters in every direction, the ground is clean. Not cleared. Clean. No glass, no wire, no ash crust, none of the two hundred years that fell on this yard along with everything else. Somebody took all of that out of this dirt by hand, a handful at a time, for longer than Panther has been switched on, on the chance that one day something might need somewhere to grow. And the rake marks go round in careful circles, and every one of them stops a hand's width short of the stem, all the way around. Whoever raked it was frightened of getting closer too.

Panther kneels.

He does not decide to kneel. There is no moment where he weighs it up. His legs simply fold under him in the mud and set him down, and the servos in them make a low sound he has never once heard them make in eighty years of service, and then he is level — amber visor slit level with two leaves, close enough that the glow coming off it stirs them.

His hands hang open at his sides. Three claws each, hardened, built to open sealed doors and hold onto brick at speed and take a running robot off his feet in the dark. There is nothing in front of him that they are the right shape for. So he does not put them anywhere. He sets them down wide apart in the mud, well back, and he is careful about it in a way nobody ever taught him and nobody is here to see him do.

Then the whole weight of it arrives at once. Two hundred years of ash. A war he was built in the middle of and has never once stood outside of. A warm room somewhere behind him with a shelf along one wall, and dozens of sleeping cores on it, and a hand-lettered name on every single one, and somebody who walks that shelf with a cloth every morning in the dark with no proof that any of them are ever coming back. Out past the fence line, out in the valley, a strip-miner the size of a mountain sitting with its lamps banked and its treads cooling, waiting for the sky to go gray so it can start.

All of it. Every burned street, every argument at every gate, every name on that shelf. About this. About something smaller than his hand that is trying very hard to stay standing up in the rain.

The wind swings around. He shifts his shoulder into it. He does not notice doing it — there is no order, no thought, no decision anywhere in the movement — and the rain that was falling on the sprout runs off his back instead, off the panther-head helmet in ropes, and lands in the mud a meter away. In the sudden dry of his shadow the two leaves lift slightly. Panther holds exactly where he is, and takes the storm on his own plating, so as not to give it back to them.

And holds. One second. Two. Four whole seconds, which is longer than that scanner has needed for anything in his entire service, because in eighty years he has never pointed it at a single thing it did not already have a word ready for. Rubble. Alloy. Ash. Water. Tonight it is doing arithmetic nobody ever expected it to have to do.

LIVING.

And in the wet orange plating of his own forearm, small and perfectly clear, an ember-lit visor slit shows on the ridge behind him, holding absolutely still.

### `n20` THE STOLEN PROOF

*from: `n13`*

**He runs off with the only living thing on Earth cupped in his claws.**

It takes less than a second. Three claws go in under the sprout, down past the roots, and lift it out of the clean circle soil and all in one motion — a neat job, the kind of job he is famous for on his own side of the war — and Panther is over the wire fence and gone into the rain before a single lantern in that yard has finished turning toward the sound.

Nobody chases him.

That is the part that gets in. No shout. No whistle. Nobody swings a rake at his ankles, nobody sounds anything, nobody so much as runs three steps for the look of it. Fifty Wardens stand exactly where they were standing, in the rain, in a ring around a small dark hole, and they watch him go. One of them lifts a lantern higher as he crosses the fence line — not to see him better, he works out later, but to light the top rail so he does not catch a heel on it. He is two gardens away before he understands why his plating is crawling. They were never guarding it from him. They were guarding it from the weather. And he is the weather now.

He runs one-armed. Two blocks of wet tile and dead aerials, the other claw curled over the plant like a roof, tilting it out of the wind at every jump, turning his shoulder into every gust so that not one drop of acid rain gets past him onto two leaves. ### `n23` REFUSAL

### `e_captain` CAPTAIN — ENDING (BAD)

*from: `n22`*

**Nothing burns. Nobody falls. He wins the night.**

Rain rings on the gantry grate and the furnace light comes up through it in bars, and Panther stands in the same cage of orange he stood in when the order was given to him, and describes an empty yard.

Bare dirt. Standing water. A fence with a tricycle rusted into it. Nothing growing anywhere in it, nothing under it worth two meters of anybody's morning. He says it levelly, the way he says everything, without a pause anywhere he could be asked about later.

Titan's colossal back fills the whole sky in front of him — soot-streaked, rain steaming off the shoulders where it lands, yellow pauldrons beaded silver — and it never turns around. Not once. It does not need to. In eighty years Titan has not had a single reason not to believe him, and now he never will, and that is the machinery of the thing: the trust was built patiently, over decades, by a robot who told the truth every time, precisely so that it would hold the one night it was used.

The amber slit does not flicker. Furnace light slides across it and finds nothing behind it at all.

"Thank you, Panther," Titan says. And then, quieter, not turning, more to the valley than to him: "I wanted it to be there."

Below them the crucibles tip on schedule. The smoke goes sideways in the wind. The report is filed and closed inside a minute, and nothing in the whole hollowed hill so much as changes note.

Dawn comes up thin and the rain thins with it to mist. On that same gantry a precise ice-blue figure walks out of the furnace glare, unhurried, and stops in front of him at exactly the distance a courteous robot stops.

Ice-blue fingers press a captain's mark onto the orange chest plate and smooth it flat with one thumb. The badge seats with a small click, and the click is the loudest thing that happens all morning.

"Captain," Samurai says. That is all he says. The horned crest tips a fraction, and he walks away down the grating, and that is the entire ceremony. Nobody says well done. Nobody has to. Rain beads on the fresh badge and runs off it in clean lines and does not mark it at all.

Panther does not look down at it.

Far away and far below, the sprout is still down there. Two leaves ticking under the rain at the exact centre of forty clean metres that somebody scrubbed by hand for two hundred years, in a yard with all the lanterns gone out of it. The Wardens stood in their ring all night waiting for a machine that never came over the ridge, and at grey light they went in out of the wet, puzzled and quietly grateful, and hung their lanterns back on their hooks. So the sprout gets knocked flat by a drop, and comes back up, and gets knocked flat again, and comes back up again, growing quietly in the dark with nobody watching it, waiting a hundred years for somebody else to walk past that fence and look through it. Nobody is coming for it.

Nothing was destroyed tonight. Nobody de-rezzed. No wall came down, no torch was lit, no core went onto anybody's shelf with a fresh tag on it, and not one light anywhere in this district went out. That was the entire offer and it was kept to the letter. Nothing was risked and nothing burned and nothing happened at all, and it cost the only thing on Earth that was still trying.

The rung goes. Bolt's small navy hand skids off wet steel, the ladder hands his weight straight back to him, and there is nothing under his feet at all. He does not shout. Every lantern in the world is on the far side. Every helmet on the command deck is facing forward.

Orange plating that was not there a heartbeat ago is there now, braced flat along the hull, cyan strips flaring up the length of the arm and stuttering with the strain, three claws locked and not giving a millimetre. Anybody would drop what they were carrying. Bolt does not. The other fist stays clamped shut around the dented sage-green tin the whole way down and the whole way back up, lid down, the faded flower label pressed hard against his chest. Panther notices that before he notices anything else about him. Eighty years of reading what robots do with their hands when the load comes on, and this is the one that goes in and stays in.

Panther does not answer. He hauls instead, and it is not graceful. Servos load up and complain. The ladder rings once, hard, under the both of them. He only made sure it could happen. There is a difference between those two things, and he has spent his entire life on the wrong side of it, and tonight, for about four seconds, he was on the other one.

**He holds the line. The line does not save the kid.**

Panther holds the line. Tray of wet dirt in both claws, amber visor locked forward on the wall of treads, rain sheeting off his shoulders and down the backs of the small dented robots pressed in on either side of him. The ground-shake comes up through fifty pairs of feet at once. The line does not move. He tells himself, once, that this was never his war — and it works. That is the worst part. It works.

His claws tighten on the tray rim until the thin tin buckles and folds under them with a small crumpling sound. Nothing else about him moves at all. "Easy," the little one says. "It's only dirt."

There is only a set of fingers opening on wet steel, fifteen meters up, in front of exactly one robot in this valley who was built fast enough to do something about it and is choosing, right now, with his feet planted, not to.

The rain takes Bolt away from the moving hull in a slow tumble, arms out, antennae turning over and over against the grey. It looks gentle. It is not gentle. It is only far away.

It blooms out into the storm, silent and blue-white, wide as a house for half a second, and folds quietly away again — a small cold firework that nobody in the world wanted. After it there is nothing in the air but falling drops, and one dormant core coming down gently among them, its light already sinking to the slow sleeping blue of a robot who will have to wait for parts. Down the line, the lanterns that saw it dip. Nobody says anything. There is nothing you say.

The tin bounces off the rim of a tread with a bright little ring, absurdly cheerful, the only clean note in the whole morning. It spins once through the wet dust.

It rolls to a stop in the mud with the faded flower label turning face down, and the next tread wall grinds past and throws grit over the lid, and that is the last of it. Whatever the kid spent all night rehearsing to say up on that deck — checking the same seeds every eight seconds, starting the sentence again from the beginning every time he got to the end of it — nobody on that deck ever hears one word of it.

By noon the yard is gone. The wall, the grass, the perfect scrubbed circle, the vault sleeping unopened underneath it, all of it under two meters of turned ground, and the crawler moving on west exactly on schedule with its dust settling behind it. Panther is still standing in the tread ruts when the last of it goes quiet. He is still holding the buckled tray. Then he walks out across the churned ground, alone, and picks a cold blue core out of the dust with three claws that have never once been the right shape for anything gentle, and wipes the mud off it with the flat of his thumb, and carries it east.

In Haven that evening, under lantern light, an old dented Warden climbs a step stool at the long polished shelf and sets a fresh hand-lettered tag beside a new core. He does not ask who brought it in. He wipes the tag twice with his cloth, though there is no dust on it yet, and squares it up on its bent wire.

"You'll keep," he tells it.

Then he stays. He does not go back to work and he does not sit down; he stands on the stool with the cloth in his hand and looks at the row for a while, and the row looks back at him — dozens of sleeping cores glowing faint cyan the whole length of the wall, each with a name on it, each one dust-free, because somebody in this building walks this wall every single day and refuses to give any of them up. He will be back tomorrow. He will be back the day after that, and the year after that, for as long as it takes somebody to spare the parts.

Out in the doorway, in the rain, an orange shape that never came inside turns and goes. Behind him the lanterns burn on down the shelf, and the newest tag is still swinging, very slightly, on its wire.
